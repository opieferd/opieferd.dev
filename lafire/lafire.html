When it happens — the countdown

  • T-4 to T-3 daysNWS issues a Fire Weather Watch (up to 96 h ahead). City EOC and departments hold an adverse-weather coordination call ~24 h before conditions arrive.
  • T-2 to T-1 daysRed Flag Warning (≤ 48 h ahead). LAFD requests state-reimbursed pre-positioning through Cal OES; the state stages its own strike teams in Southern California counties.
  • Day before, 3:00 PMLAFD fire-weather tactics conference call reviews the Burning Index, wind forecast and the pre-deployment matrix. Augmentation notice goes out ~4:45–5:00 PM (Jan 6, 2025: 16:45).
  • Morning of wind onsetExtra engines physically posted at hillside-area stations (historically by 8:00 AM), formed into 3-engine task forces and 5-engine strike teams, plus brush patrols, water tenders, dozers and helicopters.
  • During the event (24–72 h)Deployment typically held ~48 h and re-evaluated daily; crews may hold over on shift ("limited recall") to double staffing — the step LAFD skipped on Jan 7, 2025.

Season: most pre-deployments cluster in the Santa Ana window, roughly October–February, but they happen any time offshore wind + dry fuel line up (e.g., April 4, 2026).

How far away does the fire have to be?

There is no distance rule — the fire doesn't exist yet. Pre-deployment is triggered by forecast probability, not proximity: crews are staged 12–24 hours before the wind arrives so a first-alarm force is already inside the hazard zone when an ignition happens. The bet is on where fires start, not where one is burning.

Once a fire is burning, staging works differently. Wind-driven embers ignite homes "from over a mile away" (Chief Crowley), and Santa Ana fires can run several miles in hours — so during an active fire, engines stage ahead of the projected path in threatened neighborhoods, and mutual-aid engines "move up" to backfill emptied stations citywide. During the Jan 20, 2025 PDS event, LAFD held pre-deployed task forces at Stations 59, 77, 82 and 84 even while the Palisades Fire was still burning.

Rule of thumb: weather forecast ≈ 1–4 days out → decision; wind onset ≈ hours out → engines in place; active fire ≈ 1–5 miles out (ember + spread range) → evacuation and path-of-fire staging.

Who decides, and what tips the decision

  • LAFD's pre-deployment matrix weighs the Burning Index, recent rain, overnight humidity recovery, forecast wind, regional fire activity, the GACC outlook, aircraft availability and competing hazards.
  • Money shapes the call. In 2017 the matrix was softened from "shall staff" to "consider staffing" extra engines, for budget reasons — on Jan 7, 2025 that left 40+ engines and ~225 available members unused. State reimbursement via Cal OES pre-positioning requires a request and approval; a fuel-moisture/humidity error on the Jan 2025 worksheet meant only ⅓ of eligible resources were activated.
  • Command judgment. The Deputy Department Commander runs the tactics call; the Fire Chief owns the posture. Former battalion chief Rick Crawford: ~25 pre-deployed engines was past practice for major events; on Jan 7 only 5 of the 40+ available "200-series" engines were staffed and a ~1,000-firefighter holdover was declined.
  • Since the after-action review (released Oct 8, 2025), Chief Jaime Moore (appointed Nov 2025) says a PDS alert now triggers automatic resource surges, and names Baldwin Hills, Franklin Canyon, the Hollywood Hills, the Palisades, Porter Ranch and Sunland-Tujunga as the areas that must be covered simultaneously.
  • County & state layers: LA County Fire runs an "augmented staffing plan" (extra staffing + ground/air pre-deployment countywide); Cal OES and CAL FIRE preposition strike teams, engines and aircraft across LA, Ventura, Orange and Riverside counties ahead of forecast events (Jan 19, 2025: 98 OES engines, 76 CAL FIRE engines, 8 helicopters staged regionally).

How to read a response clock

A fire response is a chain of measured intervals. LAFD logs them as call processing (911 call entered in dispatch → first unit dispatched), turnout (station alert → wheels rolling), and travel (en route → first unit on scene). The NFPA 1710 urban benchmarks: dispatch in ≤ 64 seconds, turnout in ≤ 80 seconds, first-engine travel in ≤ 4 minutes — roughly a 5½-minute call-to-arrival standard for 90% of incidents.

Wildfires stretch every link of that chain. The dispatch clock still runs in seconds (Palisades: about one minute), but travel is governed by terrain — the ~7-minute estimate to the Palisades dispatch address became ≈19 minutes to the actual origin up a single mountain road. And two clocks that barely exist for a structure fire end up deciding the outcome: time-to-evacuation-order and time-to-containment. Those are the clocks this page compares.

What the clocks say

  • Dispatch was never the failure. Where logs exist, call-to-dispatch ran in about a minute and crews rolled immediately — inside NFPA-benchmark territory even on the worst day.
  • The fatal minutes were alerting minutes. The Palisades' first order came 98 minutes after the 911 call, ~40 minutes after homes were burning; west Altadena's came 9 hours in. Both after-action reviews center on that clock, not the driving clock.
  • Force-in-place beat force-en-route. Sunset (day-2 surge posture) died at 43 acres in ~22 hours; Hughes matched the big fires' spread rate but ended with nothing destroyed. The siege's response-time lesson is mostly a pre-deployment lesson — see page 1.
  • Wind caps the response ceiling. Eaton's helicopters reached the fire in 18 minutes and were grounded 9 minutes later. Past ~60 mph gusts, no arrival time compensates.
